Yes, you've got the basic concept of expected value correct. It's calculated by multiplying each possible outcome (Xi) by its probability (Pi) and summing these products:

E[X] = X₁×P₁ + X₂×P₂ + ... + Xₙ×Pₙ

Let me explain the examples more clearly:

## Risk Assessment Example

Insurance companies use expected value to set premiums. For instance:

Imagine a home insurance policy where:
- There's a 1% chance of a $10,000 fire damage claim
- There's a 0.1% chance of a $100,000 total loss claim
- There's a 5% chance of a $2,000 water damage claim

The expected cost to the insurance company is:
(0.01 × $10,000) + (0.001 × $100,000) + (0.05 × $2,000) = $100 + $100 + $100 = $300

The company would charge more than $300 for this policy to cover their expected costs plus make a profit.

## Gambling Decision-Making Example

Let's clarify with your specific example:

You put $10 on the table and say "if I roll a 6 on a die, I get double my money."

Here's what happens:
- If you roll a 6 (probability 1/6): You get $20 back (your original $10 plus another $10)
- If you roll 1-5 (probability 5/6): You lose your $10

The expected value calculation is:
- Gain if you win: +$10 (you put in $10 and get $20 back)
- Loss if you lose: -$10 (you lose your $10 bet)

Expected value = (1/6 × $10) + (5/6 × -$10) = $1.67 - $8.33 = -$6.67

This negative expected value (-$6.67) means that if you played this game many times, you would lose about $6.67 per game on average. This is why casinos remain profitable - almost all games have a negative expected value for players.

The $1.67 figure I mentioned earlier only looked at the potential gain, but didn't account for the cost of playing, which gives an incomplete picture. The complete expected value needs to include both potential gains and losses.
